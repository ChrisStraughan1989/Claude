IR & ER Update

The three biggest industrial and employee relations developments this quarter, and what they mean on the ground.

Annual Wage Review 2026: award rates up 4.75%, minimum wage breaks $1,000/week

+4.75%Increase to modern award minimum rates, effective from the first full pay period on or after 1 July 2026
$26.44/hrNew National Minimum Wage: $1,004.90/week, the first time it has topped $1,000

The Fair Work Commission also began phasing out the lowest award classification (C13), restructuring the bottom of the award pay scale. Worth tracking if your rates sit near that level.

What this means for you: if you haven't already updated pay rates for 2026/27, this is overdue and accruing risk with every pay run. We can run a same-week rate check across your award(s).

Payday Super starts 1 July 2026

Super guarantee contributions must now be paid at the same time as wages and land in the employee's fund within 7 business days of payday. A new "qualifying earnings" measure replaces ordinary time earnings. Missing the window can trigger ATO penalties and interest, even where unintentional.

What this means for you: payroll cut-offs and super clearing house timing need to be re-checked against the new 7-day rule; this is a process risk, not just a finance one.

Reform on the horizon

The Government has launched the first-ever full review of the National Employment Standards, the Commission is examining regulated labour hire arrangements, and award flexibility in hospitality and retail is under active review. Meanwhile, "Same Job, Same Pay" orders continue to land, with orders and matters under consideration worth more than $200 million nationally. None of this changes your obligations today, but it's the clearest signal yet that further reform is coming.

Section 02

Award Compliance Corner

Award underpayment remains one of the most common, and most expensive, risks we see in WA businesses. Here's what to check this quarter.

1. Rates

Confirm every classification reflects the 4.75% increase from the first full pay period on or after 1 July 2026, including allowances and penalty rates, which are often missed.

2. Casual conversion

80+ modern awards now carry a casual conversion / "employee choice" pathway. Long-term regular casuals may trigger active obligations to respond to conversion requests.

3. Classifications

With the FWC phasing out the C13 classification, businesses using entry-level classifications should watch for flow-on structural changes in future reviews.

4. Rostering & overtime

Hospitality and retail award flexibility is under active FWC review, with potential changes to penalty rates and rostering rules worth watching if these awards apply to you.

Real result: we recently helped a 50+ employee sporting club that had been paid under the wrong award for five years, correcting classifications, contracts and payroll, and protecting directors from future liability.
Section 03

WHS & Safety Focus

Two WHS developments worth putting on your radar this quarter, particularly for WA-based employers.

Psychosocial hazards: guidance is now enforcement

Psychosocial hazards (bullying, excessive workload, fatigue, poor workplace behaviour and emotional harm) are now a regulated, enforceable obligation in every Australian jurisdiction. PCBUs must actively identify, assess and eliminate these risks, not just respond after a complaint. Psychological injury claims already cost employers more than double the average physical injury claim.

New exposure limits for hazardous chemicals from 1 December 2026

New workplace exposure standards for hazardous chemicals take effect on 1 December 2026. If your business handles hazardous chemicals, dust or airborne contaminants, your safety documentation and monitoring should be reviewed well ahead of this date.

What this means for you: a documented psychosocial risk assessment is no longer "nice to have," and chemical exposure controls should be reviewed now, not in the weeks before December.

Respect@Work Spotlight: the Positive Duty has teeth

The obligation to prevent sexual harassment before it happens is no longer theoretical. It's an active enforcement priority: the Australian Human Rights Commission has confirmed six formal inquiries underway, a further 18 organisations being monitored, and roughly 140 complaints relating to potential non-compliance. A statutory review of the positive duty is also scheduled this year, putting employer conduct under a brighter spotlight than ever before.

What this means for you: the positive duty requires proactive, documented prevention (policies, training, risk assessments and clear reporting pathways), not just a reactive complaints process. Waiting for a complaint before acting is now itself a compliance gap.
